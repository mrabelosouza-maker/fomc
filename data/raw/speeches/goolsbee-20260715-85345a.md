# Austin Goolsbee — Fireside Q&A Transcript

## 1. Career path and how he ended up at the Chicago Fed

**Moderator:**

Rather than going through your bio, which obviously we know is really interesting, I think it'd be great to hear from you about your career path and how you ended up in this job.

**Austin Goolsbee:**

Oh geez. I was born in Waco, Texas. My dad's family is from Waco. My mom's family is from Abilene. They moved to California when I was a kid.

When I was in high school, I liked public affairs and speech and debate, and I liked math and science. I was uninformed, but I wasn't wrong, that it seemed like economics was sort of a combination of the debate team and math and science.

So I got into that. I went to college and then got a PhD, and then my first job was at the University of Chicago Business School. I fell madly in love with the Midwest. I had never spent any time in the Midwest, and then 30 years later I kind of woke up and realized this is home.

The University of Chicago, for somebody in economics, I used to say it was kind of like Disneyland was for my kids when my kids were 10 years old. If you're an economist, it's amazing. The university is amazing. The city, the region, lovely.

A guy that I knew mostly from birthday parties was named Barack Obama and was a teacher at the University of Chicago. It's not every day that somebody you knew from the neighborhood becomes the president.

But I went for the first three years of the Obama administration. I was at the Council of Economic Advisers. I came back to the university.

And then the Chicago Fed — there's mandatory retirement at age 65. So you might have met Charlie Evans, the previous president, and his birthday was public knowledge, so he was leaving.

I missed the mission-driven aspect of DC. There are a lot of things that drive you crazy about Washington and policymaking, but it's very unlike academics in that you don't wake up every week and say, “Who cares? Nobody's even paying attention. Why are we doing this?”

This job still had a foot in economics research. We have a very respected research department. Plus, it was policy. Plus, I didn't have to move. So I applied for the job and I got it. I've been there about three and a half years, and it's been lovely — for the most part.

## 2. What the Fed does — and does not do

**Moderator:**

Something that would be interesting for us to talk about is the Fed in general. The average person, going back 10 years, probably had never even heard of the Fed, but the last five, 10 years, with everything going on in the economy, it seems to be on the front page of every newspaper.

I think it would probably help all of us to know a little bit about what the Fed actually does and also what the Fed does not do.

**Austin Goolsbee:**

That's a great way to ask it.

The Fed does monetary policy, which is setting of an interest rate. It's basically setting interest rates, and we just do a short-term interest rate, and it's very kind of in the weeds.

We don't do fiscal policy, which is every other thing except the setting of that short-term interest rate. Taxes, income distribution, environmental policy, all of that type of stuff — Congress does that and the president does that.

I always say, look, our Midwest motto is: there's no bad weather, there's only bad clothing. You tell us the conditions and we'll pick the jacket, and we're going to go do the job.

What Congress does is the conditions. We have to take that. If they put in tariffs, if they change some policy, if there are wars in the Middle East, if there are big shocks to the economy, we have to process that.

The Federal Reserve Act gives us a job by law when setting the interest rate. We're supposed to maximize employment and stabilize prices, and that's it. We call that the dual mandate. That's the job. And we take that super seriously.

My observation at the FOMC meetings is everyone comes from different regions. They come from different backgrounds. They take that job really seriously. It's not about what makes the stock market happy. It's not about what makes the president happy or Congress happy. It's all about the dual mandate.

That's the most public part of the job. Every six weeks, we all fly to Washington, DC, and have an FOMC meeting, and we decide on this interest rate.

But the Fed does more than just that, especially the 12 reserve banks that are not the DC Fed.

We are bank supervisors and regulation. We don't set the policies, but we send the people out to the banks, make sure they're complying with the law and they have the capital that they say they have.

We do financial services. We are, in a very real sense, kind of a bank to the banks. So they have a master account at the Fed, and if they want cash for their ATMs, they place an order. The Bureau of Engraving and Printing prints all the money. All the cash in the economy is distributed through the banks.

And if you come through Chicago, or if you're at the Detroit branch, we have vaults with an 80,000-pound door and a dual combination. It's like our nuclear secrets. Half the people know one combination and half know the other. And we've got tens of billions of dollars in there, and we're running it in and out every day and pulling out the counterfeit.

We run $6 trillion a day of payments. Almost the majority of wired transfers, the majority of ACH direct deposit transactions — $6 trillion per day is running over the Fed rails.

And we're participants out in the community. We're supposed to be coming out to places like this and hearing from you, talking to business leaders, talking to civic leaders, what's happening in the region.

The Chicago district is 90% of Iowa, Wisconsin, Illinois, Michigan, Indiana. It's kind of the heart of the Midwest. It's the most manufacturing-intensive of all the districts. It's by far the most auto production of all the districts.

Because manufacturing tends to be among the most cyclical parts of the economy, there is a sense in which, even though everybody's fiercely proud of their own district, they are interested in what's happening in the Seventh District because we're a little bit canary in the coal mine of the business cycle.

So those are the things we do. They talk about five functions of the Fed. I gave you four. The fifth one is we're the lender of last resort and the protector of financial stability.

The Fed was created in 1913 following the panic of 1907 because people realized, hey, you better have a lender of last resort who monitors and does financial stability.

That's a little more controversial because if you ever get to the spot where you're having to be the lender of last resort, somebody screwed something up. So there's a lot of recriminations.

But that's basically what the Fed does.

When I first started, I went and looked up the polling. The inflation rate was pretty high already. I didn't do it. I wasn't there. If you were upset about 2021, that was before I got to the Fed.

A big majority of the country, 75 or something percent of people, said they were familiar with the Federal Reserve, but they did not know what it did. And then an even bigger majority said that while they didn't know what it did, they thought it was doing a bad job.

So I took some component of the job of these reserve banks — we're out in the heartland — as demystify the Fed and explain. We're not magicians. We've got economists, we've got market people, we've got business people, different people sit around the table and we're all just down there trying to do the job as is given to us by law.

And we're not the bad guys. We're the guardians of the galaxy. Our role is only on this much. The world is as big as this. Ours is on this much. But at least in that part of the world, it makes a big difference what the Fed does.

**Moderator:**

You talked a little bit about the physical money aspect of what you do. Can you tell us how we would know if any of our money actually comes from the Chicago Federal Reserve Bank?

**Austin Goolsbee:**

When you say “comes from,” that's doing a lot of work. It's on the Chicago Fed balance sheet.

If I have a one — yes, look, here's a $1 bill. If you have a $1 bill, there's a letter. And if your eyes are better than mine, you can read it and it will say what Federal Reserve Bank.

This one says G, which is the seventh letter. And if you look at it, it says Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago. So, if you have the G money, you're on our balance sheet. We've got some swag in this area. We've got the G money.

I thought you were going to say, “Can you tell if it's counterfeit?” Everybody who works in cash at the Fed has to be certified for counterfeit detection every six months. So when I got there, this is the coolest thing I've ever seen, all this cash. I was like, I need the training. So now I'm certified for counterfeit detection. I can tell you that, too.

## 4. Counterfeit money

**Moderator:**

That's good to know. I would love to hear a little bit more about that.

**Austin Goolsbee:**

One thing is you see those people lick it. Don't lick it. That's not a thing. Believe me, when the money's been in circulation long enough that it's got all of whatever is going to get on there, we shred it. You do not want to be licking it.

There are various security features. If you hold it up to the light, you should see a little watermark. Say you looked at a $100 bill, there's a little invisible Benjamin Franklin separate from the photo that you hold up to the light and you can see him.

If you have a black light flashlight and you shine it, there'll be a shiny strip that pops. If you wiggle it, the ink changes color on the hundred.

One time early in the Fed, my wife came home and she was like, “I found a $100 bill in the street.” She was really excited. She was like, “This is amazing. As somebody at the Fed, can they tell if this is real? Because I don't want to use it if it's not real.”

I was like, “Oh yeah, they can tell.” This was before I was certified.

So I came down, and the woman who's the head of cash is named Amy Alvarado. I was like, “Amy, can you tell if this is real?” And I handed it to her, and it was like this. She touched it like this: “It's fake.”

I was like, “What? What? How do you know that?”

She's like, “I can just tell by touching it. That's definitely fake.” And then she said, “If you feel it, it feels like paper. It's not supposed to feel like paper.” She's like, “The strip is supposed to be made of plastic. That's just toner, like from somebody's printer.”

And then she turned it over and printed in red all on the back were all these Chinese characters and it said, “Do not copy.” And she was like, “You didn't even look at this.”

So that's how you know it's Chicago money. And that's how you know it's real money.

## 5. Goolsbee's actual job and the regional Fed structure

**Moderator:**

In sharing a little bit about what's going on at the Fed and what the responsibilities are, you talked about how part of what you do is go out and meet with communities and businesses to hear what's going on. Tell us more about your actual job and what your day-to-day job looks like because I'm sure it's different every day.

**Austin Goolsbee:**

Sometimes that morphs into: why does Chicago have a Fed? Why is it different from every other government agency?

To add more to the mix, the 12 reserve banks around the country are not government agencies. They're actually not-for-profits. Each one has its own board of directors made up of local business leaders, local bank leaders, local civic leaders, and they're the ones who choose the presidents of the banks.

They're not political appointees the way that the DC Fed is. The reason that exists is because when they created the Fed in 1913, just like today, people were deeply uncomfortable that the federal government or the federal government plus the Wall Street banks would collectively control the entire U.S. financial monetary system with no input from the rest of the country.

So they created a system that automatically has regional independence in it, comprised of people who are coming from throughout the country.

If we get into the real weeds of how does the voting work, it's a rotating mix. There's 19 people around the table — the seven DC people and the 12 reserve banks — but only five of the 12 reserve banks are voting at any given time.

Yes, it's a little bit cludgy, but that regional aspect has proven to be a little bit of a stroke of genius. And unlike the First National Bank of the United States and the Second Bank of the United States, this one has stuck and lasted. I think in large measure it's because you have automatic representation from the regions.

So then you say, what does the bank president do? You're the CEO. A little bit less than 2,000 people work at Chicago Fed. There's 1,500 of them at the Chicago headquarters. Then there's a Detroit branch, and we've got a Des Moines office with mostly bank supervision, and then some folks sprinkled out throughout the district because there's banks all over the place.

Part of the job of the president is a management job: making sure we're supervising banks, making sure that we're providing financial services in a compelling way. If you got as much money as we got, we got a law enforcement unit. We've got our own police force.

Then getting out and being an upstanding member of the community and hearing from the business and other leaders.

Plus, every six weeks, go to Washington DC. So of any six-week period, let's call it two of the weeks are preparing for and going to the FOMC meeting, doing monetary policy. And then the other four weeks are managing the functions of the Fed that all the employees are doing, or getting out and talking to people and hearing about conditions.

## 6. CPI, inflation and the path toward 2%

**Moderator:**

I'm assuming that data analysis and understanding is a big part of what you're doing. Today obviously is a really big day from that standpoint. When we were connecting with your team over the last couple of weeks, they shared with us that today is a really big day in the world of the Fed and really just in the world of economics. It is the day that the consumer price index comes out for the month.

**Austin Goolsbee:**

Yeah, the inflation came out this month. I don't know if it's like Fourth of July for us. That's a big day, but it comes every month.

If you didn't see it, the CPI came out this morning and for this month it was surprisingly benign. Inflation was lower than what it has been running, and that was nice. It was a bit cutting the other way from what things have been for the last six months or so.

But I always say one month is no months. You never want to overreact to one month. If we got several months like this, I would be feeling better.

What I've been highlighting for the last several months is, if you break out inflation into components — housing, goods, services — the services thing is the one that's made me the most nervous.

You could convince yourself that tariffs should be a one-time increase in prices, but not a lasting inflation rate increase. That should be one and done.

But services aren't affected by tariffs that much, and they're not affected by the price of oil that much. So the fact that services inflation has been way too high — historically, services inflation is quite persistent — that's what I've been highlighting. That number has been making me nervous.

At least today, at least for this one, the services inflation was very encouraging.

Not to get too far into the weeds, CPI inflation is one measure of inflation. It's not exactly the measure we usually look at. We look at a broader measure called PCE inflation, which includes some other prices. We've got to see what those producer prices, how their inflation is doing, and we'll get that tomorrow.

But if we got PCE versions of inflation that look like what the CPI inflation was today, and we got that for several months, and it was clear we're back on a path toward 2% inflation, which is our target, I would be feeling a lot better.

I felt like before we got some of what I was calling the stagflationary dust in the air, we were on path to get to 2%. And the sooner we can show that we're headed back to 2%, I think the sooner we can normalize.

## 7. Interest rate outlook and labor market assessment

**Moderator:**

It's hard to have a crystal ball, obviously, especially with all the volatility and the change that's happening in the world right now. But what should we expect this year when it comes to the interest rate and what the future's looking like here?

**Austin Goolsbee:**

That's a big question. That's a huge question.

I've never been a huge fan of forecasting here's what the interest rate is going to do, even at the next meeting. I don't like even implicitly tying our hands or putting weight on the scale. I want to see what the data are coming in, and I want to hear from the other of the 19 people sitting around the table because I have found it to be — people take the job very seriously and it's a wonderful deliberative body.

It doesn't mean we still don't make mistakes because, like I say, we're not magicians. But it's a deliberative body.

That said, I think that on the job market side, the fear in the middle of last year, expressed by a bunch of folks publicly, was that maybe the job market was falling apart, was deteriorating.

My read of the job market is it's been remarkably stable without being good. So it's not a super boom, but it's not falling apart. Almost all the measures have been pretty stable.

The thing that was, I think, a little misleading is there was a time when, if you asked an economist, what's the thing, if you could only look at one number to tell you where we are in the business cycle, how the job market is doing, they would have told you: take the monthly jobs created number, payroll employment. Take that number. That's the best number.

That has broken down as the leading indicator of the business cycle. It's not working because there are major question marks about population growth. A lot coming from immigration, some coming from labor force participation and the elderly.

And now I'm going to count over age 55. Traditionally, it had relatively low labor force participation. That thing is declining, declining. Choices that people over age 55, let's call it 55 to 75, are making about whether they want to work longer — those make a huge difference in how many jobs are created.

We had 2023, 2024 really big jobs-created-per-month numbers, which some people interpreted as we're about to overheat. I started saying then, hey, wait a minute, that's not a great indicator of the business cycle because we're having a ton of immigration that you don't know that's adding to the population.

And now we've seen the reverse side. We've had relatively weak objective numbers of jobs created, but the unemployment rate has been stable and all the rates-based measures — the hiring rate, the layoff rate, the vacancy rate, the unemployment rate — those have been largely flat. And I think those are the best indicators of the business cycle.

I still feel like unless we're getting big shocks coming from wars, pandemics — a lot of things have gone wrong over the last five or eight years — the through line has been stability in the job market. It doesn't feel like a super boom.

Anybody who's got teenage kids or kids coming out of college, as I do, you know, the hiring rate is very low. If you just took the hiring rate, it's as low as it is at the depths of a recession. And so, of course, new graduates are going to feel that the most because they're disproportionately the new hires.

But at the same time, the layoff rate is so low that you would think we're at the peak of a giant boom, that people aren't getting laid off and people aren't getting hired. It's worth noting that's a weird combination. That's not a normal business cycle combination.

I think it's because there's a lot of uncertainty and so businesses just don't want to let go the people. It was hard enough to get the people they got, but they don't want to bring on new people because they're like, well, what if everything switches?

I feel like on the job side we're still going to see that.

On the inflation side, we've had more of a problem. We were making progress from 3%, getting down and down. We were close to 2% for a 10-month period, let's call it. Then we stopped making progress.

The tariffs came in. That was supposed to go away. We were kind of waiting for that increment to go away. Then the war started, the price of oil went up before that one had gone away.

So we stopped making progress. It was coming down, then it stopped going down, and then lately it's been going the wrong way. Inflation is way too high, going the wrong way.

Now we've got to start having a conversation about is inflation going to be more persistent than what we wanted it to be, than what the forecast was going to be. That for me has been the thing I've been trying to grapple with, and why I've been as nervous about services inflation as I have, because usually if you got services inflation, that's not going away quickly.

I'm heartened by this CPI today, but we need a lot more than one month to think that it's going well.

## 8. Demographics, labor supply and the Midwest

**Moderator:**

I'd like to dig a little deeper on some of the comments you made about the general population and demographics and workforce. Something we've been talking a lot about here in Kenosha County is the demographic cliff. We're kind of on the precipice of that.

We've been talking for a long time that the baby boomers are starting to retire, the silver tsunami is coming, the younger generations are smaller than past generations. We also have obviously a decrease in immigration, at least the last year or so, with new policies.

So we're really starting to have conversations here around how do we tackle that, because we have all these great businesses in Kenosha County, but we don't have population growth, really through birth rates or through net migration from other states or regions.

Do you have any thoughts at all on what that demographic shift looks like in the Midwest as a whole? What you're seeing as some good solutions to that?

**Austin Goolsbee:**

Well, let's go to the first part. I don't know the solutions.

This demographic challenge, population challenge, has characterized much of the Midwest. Population growth's been slower than the rest of the country, and that's not a new phenomenon. Ever since they invented air conditioning, there's been a drift to warmer Sun Belt locations because they became livable in the summertime.

Wisconsin, I think the unemployment rate in Wisconsin is like 3.4%. So Wisconsin is kind of in labor-shortage mode. If you talk to business people, a lot of them say, I can't find people. It never actually got better. We were in an unbelievably tight labor market and it's still a really tight labor market.

This, I do think — you called the silver tsunami, the retirement of the baby boom — and before are they Gen Z, whatever is the reflection of the baby boom. Actually, it's even bigger than the baby boom, but there's going to be a big dip for several decades in between.

And while that happens, we've got to hope that the young people aspire to careers and can replace the folks that are retiring.

If you talk to the people that are retiring, they're less optimistic about it than the people coming in. But before you get too worried about that, the one thing they're expert at at the University of Chicago is cuneiform writing, where they punch in the clay tablets.

I once talked to a guy who's a world expert on it. He can read this thing. I said, “What do they write about? What did they write?”

And evidently a lot of it was accounting. It was like you have 12 sheep and we owe you. Some of it was kings saying how great they were. There's love poems. And then there's old people complaining about young people: they abandon the traditions.

So I think it's still going to be fine. The technology is changing and maybe one upside is, if there is a rise of AI that enhances the productivity of the people where they are in the jobs, maybe that can span a little bit of the demographic bust. But that's a challenge facing the whole Midwest. There's no two ways around it.

Most of the solutions move you into fiscal policy. The Fed is kind of about the next six weeks, the next six months, where are we in the business cycle.

If it's like, how do we invest in our workforce training so that 20 years from now young people today are well set up, that's got to be the states, the Congress, and the businesses themselves.

In a way, I'm encouraged by that because the success of the economy does not rely on some government bureaucrat deciding who should be working in what job. Everybody has an incentive to find jobs and get the training for something that's going to be a meaningful career. And the businesses want those people, and we've got to kind of rely on some market mechanisms there.

## 9. Manufacturing, supply chains and the I-94 corridor

**Moderator:**

When we talk to our business community here, it's largely manufacturing, supply chain, logistics, which I'm sure you're well aware of. You drive on that I-94 corridor. You've seen all the development that's happened over the last 20 or so years.

We really benefit from that. We've got a great business community here. I know you talk a lot about the value of manufacturing in our region.

How do you rate the success of our corridor and the health of it? And what do you see as some of the pressures on supply chain, logistics and manufacturing right now?

**Austin Goolsbee:**

Look, I rate us number one. We're the greatest of all the Fed districts. The numbers show it. We have the most manufacturing.

Now, that does mean that we are exposed to geopolitics and worldwide shocks and stuff more so than anybody else. So, when the tariffs came in, there was an analysis done by Brookings: what states are the most exposed to tariffs? Literally, of the seven most exposed states in the nation, four of the seven were of our five states because we've got a lot of agriculture, we've got a lot of manufacturing, the supply chain.

To some people, that's an inflation shock. These things affect just inflation. But if you're manufacturing, that's a cost shock, too.

Those supply-side shocks seem like they've gotten more frequent. If we backed up 10 years, 20 years, and you were asking economists how important is a supply chain, most people wouldn't know. They would know nothing about it.

In a way, that was one of the most important roots of the problem of Team Transitory when inflation started jumping coming out of COVID. A lot of that, I think, was coming from the supply chain being as damaged as it was and us just assuming, ah, it's just a supply chain, they'll just fix it. That takes longer. That's harder to do.

So I think those supply chain aspects are — are they a threat? I guess they're kind of a threat. The biggest vulnerability is that the last six years have taught us that those things can go wrong in a way we didn't fully appreciate.

Focusing the supply chain to be global and single-sourced because that's the cheapest, it is the cheapest, but it also is the most vulnerable when stuff goes wrong.

So I do think we're going to have to still confront those issues here in the Midwest. But we've also proved pretty resourceful.

If you look at businesses, I was down in Indiana and the RV manufacturers, they were affected by the same supply chain components that were affecting the auto industry. But they were going to try a totally different way. We're going to find workers. We're going to find people. We're going to find different forms of the supply chain which allow us to keep producing in a way that an industry as big as autos wasn't able to.

Small versus medium versus large manufacturing firms — there's a little bit of dynamic of that. It depends how big you are.

## 10. Are we living in unprecedented times?

**Moderator:**

You talk about the supply chains being disrupted in a way that we really couldn't have anticipated. I think that leads to another good question, which is I think a lot of us in this room feel like we are living in unprecedented times. Every day you have to open the New York Times or the Wall Street Journal. If you have a one-in-100-year flood every year, either stop calling it a one-in-100-year flood, or if each one is bigger than the last, maybe it is a one-in-100-year flood.

It just seems like one thing after another with wars and tariffs and pandemics, you name it. Are we living in unprecedented times?

**Austin Goolsbee:**

It feels like it. But look, that's our Midwestern sensibility. It's like, wow, this thing is terrible. Well, we've got to get out and fix that.

If the toilet stopped up, you're like, you got to fix it. What are we going to do? It's not pleasant, but that's the job.

Just back up 10 years ago. If I told you, hey, what is going to happen? We're going to have pandemics. We're going to have wars and the price of oil is going to go to $100 a barrel. You can't buy computer chips, you can't buy all of this stuff. You would have thought, oh my God, how will we fix that?

But we did. It's disruptive in the moment. And I don't mean to make light of the adjustment cost and the pain that every business and every community has had to go through in these things.

But ultimately, that adaptation in the Midwest, we've been doing that for many, many decades.

If I'd have told you we're going to have the biggest tariffs in 130 years and there would be a whole bunch of parts, components and supplies that wouldn't be exempted, then it would be whatever, April 3rd, 2024. I'm calling the manufacturing folks throughout the district. Their hair is on fire. They're like, if these tariff rates come in, we're going to die. This exceeds our entire margin.

They were able to figure out and adapt. And that's the way it has to be.

## 11. Housing affordability

**Moderator:**

One of the areas that has been really impacted by a lot of what you're talking about is housing. That's something we're talking a lot about here, affordability. I know the Fed's doing some work in that area. Can you talk a bit about that?

**Austin Goolsbee:**

Yes. House prices are way up. When economists talk about housing, we tend to talk about rent, not house sale price of housing, because when you buy a house, part of what you're getting is an asset. So it's a form of savings. The housing inflation part is just rent.

That said, it's worth noting there's been an extreme divergence in the last couple of years between house price inflation and the inflation of everything else. That's been extreme lately, but that's not new. It's actually been happening for decades.

House price inflation was three and a half, 4% a year. Goods price inflation has actually been mild deflation, like minus half a percent per year over 20, 30, 40 years.

I don't need to tell you that if you have a thing compounding plus three and a half minus what is it, 4% per year for 30 years, the relative price of buying a house versus what you can buy a TV for in Costco is going to be really different.

When young people today note, my grandpa had one salary, never went to college, bought a big house, and I went to college, did all the stuff, and I can't even afford a condo, they're not wrong. The relative price of housing's gone way up.

My inner economist is like: if you want the price of housing to slow down or even fall, you got to build more houses. Part of it has been we haven't built enough houses.

But I got to tell you, this is pervasive all around the country. We went to Cedar Rapids, Iowa, which is not a giant metro area. It's not a land-use-regulation New York City or something. I asked them in Cedar Rapids, what's the biggest challenge facing the economy of Cedar Rapids? They said price of housing is so high we can't get anybody to move here.

If in Cedar Rapids, Iowa, they're dealing with housing affordability issues, and in San Francisco and Chicago and Milwaukee and Kenosha and every other place, we do need to have a rethink of housing in the context of the price of everything else.

## 12. Most surprising and coolest parts of the job

**Moderator:**

I did a lot of studying. Every podcast I listen to, every interview I listen to with you really reminded me of a pretty iconic quote from one of my favorite movies, *Mean Girls*. When Amy Poehler, as Regina George's mom, says, “I'm not like a regular mom. I'm a cool mom.” I just kept thinking, you're not like a regular banker. You're like a cool banker.

You take these really complicated topics and you bring levity to it. You bring humor to it. So I have a couple fun questions for you, if that's all right.

Obviously, you have a really interesting job. What have you found to be the most surprising thing about that job? And what is one of the coolest things you've been able to do as a result of being in this position?

**Austin Goolsbee:**

The most surprising: I didn't know anything about the cash in the basement of the Fed. I showed up and they were like, “We got $35 billion. Do you want to see it?” I was like, “Hell yeah, I want to see it.”

So I went down. I was like, wow. And the machines are counting. I was like, this is incredible.

So that was the most surprising thing. And I still delight in taking people on tours. They had their annual evaluation report from the cash department, and they were like, we did 42 tours last year. I was like, I think 28 of them were my mom, my friends.

That was the biggest surprise and that was wonderful.

The FOMC meeting itself — the content of the meeting wasn't a surprise because it's monetary policy and if you're an economist, you've been thinking about those issues. But the physical meeting room is also really pretty neat. This giant table, and everybody sits around the table, and they put down the shades so they can't be spied on, and you're required to leave all your phones and everything out of the room like it's a situation room.

People take the job extremely seriously. They go around the table. Day one is about the economy, day two is about the rates. Jay Powell, what do you think about the economy? And you're assigned a seat. I sit between the president of San Francisco and the president of Minneapolis every time. It's really fascinating. If you're an economist, it's pretty great.

The pleasure of being back in a — if you talk to anybody at the Chicago Fed, these people are believers. They could be working in the private sector and making more money than they make at the Chicago Fed. They've chosen to do this because they absolutely believe in the mission, and that's wonderful.

And there's a lot of bureaucracy in the Fed, as you might think. I went into academics because most people become professors because they can't deal with having a boss and they can't deal with hierarchy. My wife one day was like, “You hate bureaucracy, you hate authority, why did you take a job at the Fed?”

There is a little bit of a conference of presidents and we have to work and come to consensus. It turns out consensus is really difficult.

Every FOMC meeting, they put out a collection of the anecdotal information that the banks have gathered. It's called the Beige Book. It used to be a book that was beige. Now it's a PDF file.

There was a Beige Book working group, and the Beige Book working group assigned you, like it must be a two-column format and the cover is too beige, it needs to be something else. Those kinds of big organizational surprises are less fun.

## 14. Kevin Warsh as new Fed chair

**Moderator:**

One question that was submitted by one of our audience members: obviously there's been some big news lately with a new chairman of the Fed, with Kevin Warsh being the new chair of the Fed. What are your impressions of him and where do you see him taking things?

**Austin Goolsbee:**

I don't know. I'm not going to get myself in trouble. I'm only supposed to speak for myself, not for what anybody else thinks or what the committee thinks or is going to do.

That said, I knew Chairman Warsh when he was just Kevin Warsh, Governor Warsh, back in 2009, 2010, when I was there at the beginning of the Obama administration. That's when he was on the Fed as a governor, and I worked closely with him. We would meet every month, the CEA and the Fed governors.

It felt like we went through a war together and we were in a foxhole. We came from totally different backgrounds. He was from banking. I was an academic. He was a Republican. I was a Democrat. But I got to see him in a very stressful moment, and he's levelheaded.

I've been impressed. He hasn't been there for very long. I was prone to like him as he was coming in, like I said, because I went through the financial crisis with him.

He's announced a series of task forces with people of high stature from outside the Fed to think through issues like inflation, AI and productivity, the balance sheet. I'm going to be interested to see what comes out of those because I think it's a moment where we can revisit some of the things we took for granted.

## 15. What makes him optimistic about the U.S. economy

**Moderator:**

This has been an incredible conversation. Before we wrap up, I do have one more question for you. Just as a final thought, what makes you most optimistic about the future of the U.S. economy?

**Austin Goolsbee:**

I told you that the Fed is really about the short run in the business cycle. The reasons for optimism of the economy are none of that. It's all the long run.

I got to know the investor Warren Buffett a little bit back in 2008. He was an Obama supporter and I met him at some events. And if you ever meet Warren Buffett, he has all these quizzes. He'll give you a little quiz.

He asked me, “What do you think the Dow was in 1900?” And I was like, well, there was a Dow in 1900? And the answer was 50. The Dow was at 50 in 1900.

Then he started saying, imagine if you could go back to 1899 — Kenosha was here in 1899 — and we're having the meeting in 1899, and you bring the list of everything that will go wrong in the next 127 years. You've got a hell of a list. You've got pandemics and world wars and hyperinflations, Justin Bieber, whatever is going to go wrong.

Then imagine predicting then what is the Dow going to be in 2026, and the people are going to be like, 90, maybe 90, maybe down to 40.

How do we get 30, 40, 50,000 on the Dow? The answer is because the innovative capacity of everyone in the world, but especially the American people, has proved totally unbounded. That's how we became rich.

If you plot from 1776 to the present, there are dips, there are whatever, but it's basically a straight line up.

We're going to come back in 30 years, 40 years, we're going to remember, oh man, it was such a difficult time, but it's basically going to be fine.

I don't see how you can look at that graph of per capita income in the United States for 250 years and not be a total optimist. Just go talk to some young people. It's going to work. We're going to have to figure it out. It's going to be uncomfortable, but it's going to work.

**Moderator:**

Definitely encouraging words, and we appreciate that. Thank you so much. This has been a fantastic conversation. Thank you for taking time with us.

**Austin Goolsbee:**

It's great. Thank you.
