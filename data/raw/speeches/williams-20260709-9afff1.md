## Context
Williams spoke Thursday, July 9, 2026, at a conference at the New York Fed, one day
after the release of the June 16-17 FOMC minutes (rate held at 3.50%–3.75%). He
DECLINED to say what he would decide at the July 28-29 meeting. The event came after
his Jul 7 Fox Business interview, in which he had turned "a little bit more positive"
on near-term inflation because a seeming resolution of the Middle East war was pulling
energy prices down. Since then, hostilities in the Middle East have resumed (President
Trump says the agreement that ended the hot phase is now void), re-raising the upside
risk to energy and inflation.

## Energy — benign baseline, but the resolution he leaned on has unwound
> "The markets still expect oil prices to come down over the next six to 12 months. I
> think that's a pretty reasonable baseline."

> "I still feel, kind of, the fundamentals are that energy prices are likely to be
> around their peak and then to come down over time."

Williams said he did NOT expect a sustained rise in energy prices for the rest of the
year despite the resumption of hostilities. But per Reuters, the renewed conflict
"again threaten[s] to crimp the flow of energy and other goods," and "the risks of
higher energy prices and inflation over the remainder of the year have risen,
increasing the chances the Fed may have to raise interest rates."

## Inflation — "still too high"; AI demand is now his MAIN concern
Bloomberg headline: "Fed's Williams Says AI Is Now His Main Inflation Concern."
Williams said that among the drivers of inflation in the US, he is most focused on
demand driven by artificial-intelligence investment, and that if that demand persists
it could force the central bank to raise rates. AI infrastructure investment could
lower price pressures in the future, but it is "now clearly helping fuel inflation
that he considers to be too high."

> "If this creates a sustained impulse to demand relative to supply in the inflation …
> I do think that's the kind of situation where you don't look through this. You
> basically say you need to have a monetary policy positioned … to offset the
> inflationary impulse from that."

> "A rate of core PCE of two-tenths a month in the second half of this year, that would
> be consistent with my view of a disinflationary process that's continuing. If it's
> higher than that, that would be a sign of inflation a bit more persistent."

On tariffs he was more relaxed: "There are certain parts of the inflation outlook that
are probably maybe a little bit more benign, say on the tariffs, maybe on the energy
prices, depending how that plays out."

## Reaction function — explicit two-sided conditionality, tilted to the hike leg
> "[There are] other scenarios where inflation is more persistent and stays higher,
> which would … call for tighter monetary policy. I think that's the right way to think
> about it."

> "[If inflation] ends up being more persistent and meaningfully higher than his
> baseline forecast … then monetary policy would need to respond to that. On the other
> hand, if it isn't and things play out in a more benign way, I do think monetary policy
> is, and continues to be, well positioned."

> "I think that the minutes actually captured a collective reaction function in a way,
> even though it's not designed to do that … It shows the richness of these scenarios."

Context: the June minutes showed nine policymakers penciled in at least one quarter-
point hike in 2026, and "a few" participants saw a case for a June hike; support for
hikes is growing on the committee.

## July meeting — no commitment
> "We haven't even started the process of doing an analysis."

> "We meet every six weeks. This isn't like we're making decisions forever."

## Data dependence — unchanged
> "I've spent much of my career as a policymaker talking about being data-dependent. I
> have not changed. I still think we need to be data-dependent."

## Balance sheet — reform should be about resilience, not a reduction target
Williams weighed in as Chair Warsh considers changes to the rate toolkit aimed at
further shrinking the ~$6.7T balance sheet (e.g., letting banks hold less emergency
cash). He said any change should prioritize the safety and stability of the banking
system:
> "I don't think the driver of this should be [the amount of balance-sheet reduction
> achievable]. It really should be how do we improve and make and strengthen our
> financial system."

He also called Warsh's review task forces (communications, balance sheet, inflation
models, productivity, data) a "unique and timely" opportunity, on "a pretty aggressive
timeline," and noted continued uncertainty about the longer-run neutral rate.
