**Q2 (Moderator): Tell us about your journey, how you got where you are, what you really do — and how did we come up with this 2%?**

GOOLSBEE: I was an economics PhD and professor at the University of Chicago Business School for 30 years. I had a foray in Washington, DC: President Obama was a teacher at the University of Chicago, and our oldest daughter is in between his two daughters and they were at school together — I kind of knew him as the guy from the birthday parties. When he first ran for the Senate in 2003–2004, somebody called me and asked if I would help with economics. I was at the Council of Economic Advisers at the very beginning, through the financial crisis, and came back to the University of Chicago for another eight or ten years.

The Federal Reserve banks have an important regional function as well as national — it's a policy job. There's mandatory retirement at age 65, so the previous president, Charlie Evans, had to retire. The Fed is still one foot very much in economic research plus policy, plus I'm from here; I didn't have to move to DC. So I applied for the job. It's been a weird moment for the Fed in general and for inflation — we're coming out of the first inflation in a long time — but I don't regret it. If you're an econ nerd like me, going to the FOMC room is the holiest of holies. [Anecdote: huge table, everybody goes around the room — "Jay Powell, what do you think about the economy, Chairman Kevin Warsh, what do you think about the economy" — shades down, no electronic devices; at his first meeting his second phone rang: "my Fed career was almost cut short, you know, by one Scaramucci or something."]

On 2%: the law says that when setting monetary policy the Fed must stabilize prices and maximize employment — the dual mandate. We have interpreted stable prices to mean getting the inflation rate to 2%. It should be something, but not necessarily zero, because if you aimed for zero you would be much more likely to encounter periods where the interest rate is zero, and it's hard to do monetary policy at the zero lower bound. A little something, but not so much that anybody has to think about it. And literally 2% came from somebody at the Reserve Bank of New Zealand, and everybody has congealed around it.

**Q3 (Moderator): The middle market — pressure on every side, from larger customers down to the consumer; increased labor costs, health care costs. From the Fed's perspective, how do we balance this conundrum?**

GOOLSBEE: You're getting the double sandwich squeeze: customers pushing back that the price is too high, and the cost of everything going up. You got fuel costs, you got tariffs, especially in manufacturing, you got supply chain disruption, competition from the AI data centers driving up the cost of construction.

The thing to remember is the Fed's day job is about the business cycle and it has only one tool that it can adjust, this overnight interest rate, and usually it works because the most interest rate sensitive sectors of the economy are also the most cyclical sectors of the economy. So if the economy is overheating, you raise the interest rate and try to cool it off. If the economy's chilling, you lower the interest rate and try to stimulate it. That thing of the business cycle kind of doesn't work exactly if you're facing supply shocks that are making both sides of the mandate worse at the same time. So something like the price of oil which drives up inflation and drives up unemployment at the same time, that's a central bank nightmare because it's not obvious what you do to respond to it.

If you look at manufacturing — the Chicago 7th District is the heart of the Midwest, most manufacturing of all the districts, very cyclical — demand isn't the problem right now. It's cost. Cost is the problem. And everywhere you go, you're hearing the cost of diesel is over $6 a gallon. The cost of data centers, the cost of construction, finding skilled labor, the wage pressures are high. So I kind of think the middle market is stuck in an uncomfortable spot, but it could be worse, which is when demand falls apart, then you don't hear just about costs, you hear there's no customers. And you're not hearing that.

**Q3 follow-up (Moderator): As a manufacturer — competition from China, the cost of tariffs, inconsistency in the global marketplace, higher wages, and cost-down pressure from larger customers. It's a constant squeeze.**

GOOLSBEE: The context over the last five years, nationwide, is that business margins are as big as they've ever been. When you first look at the data, people say that can't be true, this has been the most stressful environment of my career. But the margins have been there, but they're thinning. The level was high, but they're thinning, so there's tension.

On uncertainty: in April of last year, Liberation Day, they announced what the tariffs were going to be and throughout the Midwest people's hair was on fire — if you put a 30% tariff on a 12% margin, it won't work. Then they exempted USMCA-compliant goods, and we got a period of some stability. Now there is more uncertainty back again, particularly for anybody connected to Canada, as the tensions rise.

Some people have been surprised at the following observation in the labor market data: the hiring rate is as low as at the trough of a recession, very low hiring rate; the layoff rate is as low as at the peak of a boom, very low layoff rate. That's an extremely unusual combination, and I think that combination comes from uncertainty: I'm not going to get rid of anybody, but I'm definitely not going to bring new people on.

**Q4 (Moderator): AI — most businesses are trying to integrate it, on the production floor, for efficiency. How do we balance automation and AI against growing the workforce, and how do you see that shift affecting U.S. growth, employment and inflation?**

GOOLSBEE: The following is two-thirds cop-out but one-third insight. Remember that the Fed is fundamentally about the short-run business cycle. It is a deep and important question how the rise of AI or any technology is going to affect the productivity growth rate of the country. If we have higher productivity growth going forward, that's good for us — that's how we got to be as rich as we are. But that's not really the day job of the Fed. If you're asking how much productivity will go up, I'm not sure.

But I do know that in the next six months, if everyone believes that there's going to be a huge increase in productivity coming, that's a high danger to overheat the economy right now before that's happened. You've already seen the valuations of these companies going way up, and those companies saying we've got to invest trillions of dollars in building out these data centers and hiring every electrician in the state of Illinois to come build the data centers. And the thing is that's just old-fashioned overheating of the economy that the Fed tempers against. If you see the spread of that generating an inflationary impulse going through the economy, that's not a supply shock. That's something where the unemployment rate goes down and the inflation rate goes up and the Fed needs to take action to prevent that thing from spiraling. So there is a very real sense in which the bigger the AI hype about the future, the bigger the danger of the overheating in the short run. What is AI going to do to productivity? That's not my job. But if that is going to change it, then it is going to affect my job.
