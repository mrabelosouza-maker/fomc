The U.S. Economy and Monetary Policy

Thank you, Bo, for that kind introduction. There are few places I would rather be than on these beautiful grounds at the start of October. Seeing the sugar maples along the Lawn starting to turn their distinctive shade of Cavalier orange is one of this country's iconic scholastic images. I am proud to have received my Ph.D. from the University of Virginia (UVA), and I have many wonderful memories of my time spent here in Charlottesville. It is an honor to speak with you today.

This is a consequential moment for the U.S. economy and a challenging time for monetary policymakers. The economy is being shaped by the rapid adoption of artificial intelligence (AI), shifting geopolitical dynamics, and underlying demographic trends. Compounding these structural changes, the economy has also faced a cascade of shocks, including an energy shock, a massive AI infrastructure buildout, and significant changes in trade policy. I see the economy as likely to show continued resilience, despite these challenges, by adding jobs and extending a six-and-a-half-year-long expansion. However, inflation is too high and has exceeded the Federal Reserve's 2 percent target for more than five years. While I view the risks to both economic activity and employment as roughly balanced at this point, I see upside risks to inflation.

Today I will discuss in more detail how I see the economy unfolding, including for both sides of our dual mandate of maximum employment and price stability. Then, I will discuss the FOMC's policy decision last month and offer a few thoughts on how I will assess the appropriate future path of monetary policy.

## Economic Activity

Economic growth has maintained its momentum this year and has been expanding at a pace roughly in line with its potential rate despite being buffeted by several shocks. As you can see in figure 1, GDP expanded at a 2.4 percent pace in the first half of this year, roughly consistent with growth for the previous two years. Business investment has been quite strong, primarily supported by AI-related spending. Consumer spending has remained resilient despite higher energy prices and higher prices for tariff-affected products. As you can see in figure 2, spending at retailers accelerated somewhat in the first half of this year. I continue, however, to hear anecdotal evidence of elevated price sensitivity and softening consumer demand, especially for low-income consumers.

Looking ahead, I anticipate that AI-related investments will continue to support growth this year, and, accordingly, I expect near-term real GDP growth to remain roughly the same as the pace seen in the first half of the year. I view the risks to my growth forecast as roughly balanced. On the one hand, increased caution and uncertainty with heightened tensions in the Middle East may still weigh on economic activity. On the other hand, a further surge in investment spending and significant upward surprises in the AI buildout may result in GDP growth that is stronger than my baseline assumption.

## Labor Market

In the labor market, a broad range of data indicates that conditions have stabilized. Figure 3 shows that after increasing modestly in 2024 and 2025, the unemployment rate has moved roughly sideways this year and ticked down to 4.1 percent in August. I view that level as near maximum employment. Hiring picked up somewhat in the spring and summer months. In figure 4, focusing on the three-month moving average, the dashed red line, you can see hiring flattened in the second half of last year before edging higher more recently. Very slow growth in the size of the labor force allowed the unemployment rate to remain mostly stable even during that period of slower hiring. While job creation has been somewhat volatile, payroll gains have broadened to many sectors in recent months, which is encouraging. Layoffs have remained low, and job openings have moved a bit higher on net. Consistent with a stable labor market, figure 5 shows that the ratio of job vacancies to unemployed workers has moved back above 1 this year after dipping slightly below that level last year.

I expect the unemployment rate to stay around its current level through the rest of the year. In line with my growth outlook, I view the risks to my employment forecast as largely balanced. Global uncertainty, especially tied to the conflict in the Middle East, could put pressure on growth and thus weigh on the labor market. I am also closely monitoring the effects of AI on employment. It is possible that this technology leads to job losses in certain sectors or for certain groups of workers. However, if the AI buildout supports even stronger economic growth than in my baseline forecast, the unemployment rate may fall further, resulting in tighter labor market conditions.

## Inflation

Turning to the price-stability side of our mandate, I will start by acknowledging that inflation has been too high for too long. The 12-month change in the personal consumption expenditures price index, the blue line in figure 6, was 3.4 percent in August. While inflation has eased from pandemic highs, it has remained above our 2 percent target for more than five years. The 12-month change in core inflation, which excludes volatile food and energy prices, the dashed red line, was somewhat milder. The predominant factor driving the recent pickup in headline inflation is energy prices, including gasoline and diesel costs. Oil prices have been quite volatile, and global energy supplies have come under renewed strain, reflecting heightened geopolitical tensions. I remain concerned about the risk of higher energy prices leading to a persistent rise in inflation more broadly.

With respect to the components of core inflation, the category of core services excluding housing—the dashed red line in figure 7—has been edging up this year despite gradually declining nominal wage growth. The boom in AI-related demand is driving unusually strong increases in the cost of producing related goods and services, contributing to the rise in core goods prices, the solid blue line. Housing services prices, the dashed-and-dotted orange line, have been a source of disinflation in recent years, though that trend has leveled off this year.

Survey-based short-term measures of inflation expectations are elevated. Most measures of longer-term inflation expectations, however, have remained stable at levels consistent with 2 percent inflation. These developments suggest that we are not yet seeing spillovers from tariffs, energy price shocks, and the surge in certain categories of goods prices into broader and more persistent inflation. But if actual inflation remains above our target, then households and businesses may eventually stop believing that we will return inflation to 2 percent. This potential uncertainty could lead to a rise in longer-term inflation expectations and affect wage- and price-setting decisions, and I am committed to avoiding this outcome.

As my base case, I expect inflation to be elevated in the short run before resuming its decline toward our 2 percent goal as the effects of energy and other price shocks fade. I view risks to my inflation forecast as tilted to the upside due to recent geopolitical developments and stronger-than-anticipated aggregate demand.

## Monetary Policy

Considering the economic conditions that persisted through mid-September, the FOMC voted last month to raise the target range for the federal funds rate by a 1/4 percentage point to 3-3/4 to 4 percent as shown in figure 8. I supported that decision because I saw it as the appropriate policy to pursue the Fed's dual mandate. This was an important step to ensure longer-term inflation expectations remain well anchored and to validate the public's confidence that we will achieve our 2 percent inflation objective in a timely manner.

As I described, I see economic activity and labor market conditions remaining broadly solid while inflation remains above our target. The economy is being buffeted by a cascade of shocks, including rising energy prices, the surging AI buildout, and changes to trade policy. We do not have the luxury of considering each of these shocks in isolation. Rather, we must consider how this cascade might affect the entire economy when setting policy to achieve our dual-mandate objectives.

As we look ahead, my view is that any future adjustments in policy should be determined by carefully examining trends in the data, the evolving outlook, and the balance of risks. Since our September meeting, yields across the term structure have increased further, a sign that investors are reassessing the evolving macroeconomic landscape. My colleagues and I will need to come to our own judgment, which may take more time. I will continue to assess whether underlying trends suggest that inflation will return to target with sufficient speed. With more data in hand, such trends may lend themselves to better discernment, as may the appropriate stance of monetary policy.

It is the Fed's responsibility to deliver maximum employment and stable prices. That is what Congress has instructed us to do, and those are the conditions that will result in the best outcomes for all Americans.

## Conclusion

Better understanding how American families are experiencing the economy is something I studied here at UVA, and that has remained an area of focus throughout my academic career. Those lessons taught me why it is so important that the Fed restores price stability while maintaining a solid labor market. And that is what I am fully committed to accomplishing.

Thank you again for having me. It is a delight to be back in Charlottesville.

## Q&A (moderated by Darden faculty; video transcript, auto-captions, lightly cleaned)

Q: You've shared your concern about the upside risks to inflation. There has been this conventional view that well anchored inflation expectations give the Fed some room to be patient, while we also know that these inflation expectations may be well anchored precisely because the public trust the Fed to respond promptly and adequately. How do you judge when that Fed credibility is giving you room to be patient versus when that patience itself is actually putting credibility at risk?

A: It is true that inflation has been above our target for some time now and that has been in part a result of the various shocks that have hit the economy. Any responses that I give and even in my talk they are my own views and I am not speaking on the behalf of the Federal Reserve Board, the Federal Open Market Committee or the Federal Reserve system. I am of the view that the inflation that we have experienced has been a result of multiple shocks that have hit the economy, in what I called a cascade of them, repeated over time. Now with respect to inflation expectations and Fed's credibility, what I look at very closely and monitor is longer term inflation expectations. It can be the case that short-term inflation expectations do vary a lot. In fact, some people would argue that short-term inflation expectations are subject to changes in things like gas prices and diesel prices and things of that sort. But longer term inflation expectations are still consistent with the 2% inflation target set by the Fed. And in my view, that is evidence in support of the fact that the Fed is still viewed as being credible. Now, that having been said, I for a minute don't take it for granted that those longer term inflation expectations will stay around our 2% target. We have to be prepared to do the work necessary to validate those expectations. And my belief is, well I can certainly say for myself and I believe the Federal Open Market Committee is firmly committed to returning inflation to 2% in a timely manner.

Q: What are some of the measures that you might look at when you make that call that now, instead of thinking that this is the time where we can be patient to look through a supply shock, it is time to act? Are these survey based measures?

A: We have survey based measures with market-based measures where you can figure out what the implied path of inflation is going to be. Now what we have to take into consideration is the mandate given to us by Congress is not just with respect to inflation. We also have been given the mandate of maximum employment and in the statute there is no preference given to one side of that mandate versus the other. So we always have to be mindful of what's happening on the employment side. If we look at the current conjuncture of where we are right now, as I said in my remarks, my view is that we are quite close to maximum employment and have been for some time. If you look at various estimates out there in the private sector and in the public sector of what the full employment unemployment rate is, we are certainly within range of a variety of estimates. So in my mind, that's what gives us more latitude to focus on the inflation side of the mandate, in that of the two that is the side that is furthest from target and therefore, given the strength in the labor market and the resilience of the economy overall, it is my view that it's appropriate that we're focused on the inflation side at this time.

Q: How would you distinguish a genuinely very healthy labor market from one that looks stable, but where underneath hiring, layoffs and labor force growth have slowed?

A: What was notable about the labor market last year in particular was that the unemployment rate was still somewhere in the range 4.1 to 4.4%. It kind of bumped along that way for the entire year and into this year, but it was not what we would describe as a dynamic labor market in that there was not a lot of hiring going on, but there wasn't a lot of layoffs or firing going on. Whereas a healthier labor market is one in which we see more dynamism in terms of people changing jobs, the flows being much more fluid than what we've had. We've seen more of that recently, as I noted in my remarks, in terms of payrolls picking up, vacancies picking up, and so things I would say are stabilizing and even strengthening a little bit. When you look under the hood and you look beyond just the unemployment rate, what you want to be looking at are flows that suggest that there is more movement in and out of the various stages. People are willing or confident that they can take the risk of changing their job, and that job change will come typically with a higher salary. That's the kind of labor market we want to encourage.

Q: So the relatively healthy labor market, including the improved dynamism, is also part of the reason you think it's time for the Fed to be more focused on the inflation front at the moment?

A: Yes. Now, I want to be careful here, because I am aware that people are having a wide variety of experiences in the labor market. We are always doing outreach meetings with people who are participating in the economy, and we have a very large economy, different regions, different sectors. So it's rarely the case that one narrative is going to fit all of those regions, sectors, and individuals. There are still groups of workers who are facing more adversity than other groups of workers, and certainly that's something that I'm very mindful of. What I have to focus on is trying to create the kind of economy that will also generate what I think of as shared prosperity. And we know from recent economic history that the kind of economy that does that is one in which there's robust economic growth with low inflation. The expansion that ended with the pandemic was long-term growth, low inflation and broad-based prosperity, and that's very much what I as a policymaker would like us to get back to.

Q: Any other insights on the diverging economic realities that American households face these days?

A: In my current role I haven't been able to focus on areas that were the subject of my past research because my responsibilities are much broader. I have to focus on the mandates from the US Congress that have been given to the Fed and the tools that we have available to us to deliver the kind of economy that's most likely to benefit the broadest base of the American people. I think there's great wisdom in our dual mandate and if we just do that job well, that will benefit those concerns.

Q: What are the implications of AI adoption for monetary policymaking?

A: AI, the innovation and the implementation and the diffusion of this technology, could have very important impacts for monetary policy and is already having an impact. In the first instance, as I mentioned in my speech, the AI buildout is having a significant impact on the demand for investment in the United States and that is stimulating aggregate demand. With respect to the resilience to the various shocks that we have seen, a part of it is because AI is stimulating aggregate demand and allowing the economy to grow. That stimulus of aggregate demand, as a policymaker I have to consider whether or not it is being matched by the growth rate in aggregate supply, because it is the imbalance between aggregate demand and aggregate supply that feeds into the inflation that we see. Then there are the labor market implications: some jobs will benefit from the introduction of AI and some will not, and may even in the transition disappear, so that can generate some concerns about what's happening in the labor market in the short term. Longer term, AI holds the promise of increasing productivity in the economy, and increased productivity growth is for a monetary policymaker like manna from heaven. It makes our jobs so much easier given the tools that we have. There is the promise that there will be tremendous productivity gains, allowing US productivity growth to be 2% or even possibly more going forward, and that raises living standards for everyone. So in the short run we are currently dealing with the aggregate demand effects of the AI buildout; in the longer run, potentially very positive aggregate supply impacts that will help with respect to monetary policy; and we always have to be mindful of the labor market implications, knowing that in the longer run there will be jobs and industries created that we have not even imagined today. I would very much encourage responsible development of AI, but I think it's very important for the US economy that we have this innovation and that we stay on the cutting edge of it.

Q: What evidence would sway you that r*, the natural rate of interest, has shifted because of AI?

A: If there were sustained evidence that productivity growth was increasing from its long run average, then that would be very persuasive. Over long periods of time, average productivity growth in the United States is about one and a half percent per year. If AI is just going to be the thing that keeps our productivity growth at one and a half percent per year, that's good, because we need something to do that. But if AI is going to cause average productivity growth for the next 50 years to be two to two and a half percent, that's a different world from my baseline expectation. With that productivity growth, I would expect increased investment demand, and households expecting higher future income, so the savings rate might go down a little bit. So you have greater investment demand, a lower savings rate, a higher long run equilibrium real interest rate in the economy, and of course that would have implications for monetary policy. If the long run equilibrium real interest rate is higher, then that necessarily impacts how any given level of the policy rate is interpreted as restrictive or not. My mind is still open about all of this, because it could also be the case that AI innovation could cause more income inequality, and high income households have a lower marginal propensity to consume, so there could be more savings, and that could moderate any increase in r* in the long run. So right now it's too soon to say what the impact of this might be on r*. But in the meantime we have to make monetary policy and that's what we are trying to do.

Q: Are you concerned about vulnerabilities in private credit and its linkages with traditional banking?

A: The non-bank financial sector is huge, something on the order of 126 trillion dollars. What we care about very much is the buildup in risk anywhere in the economy; part of our responsibility is financial stability. Banking organizations have done some lending to the private credit sector, and we have lines of sight into the counterparties to the private credit firms, even though we don't have great lines of sight into what private credit ultimately does. Our responsibility is to make sure that the banks are carefully monitoring the risk that they're taking on by lending to private credit. We have financial surveillance of the activities of the banks that we oversee and we have provided guidance in our supervision and regulation capacities to make sure the banks are appropriately calibrating their risks. This is a highly evolving part of our work.

Q: What is your view on the appropriate long-run size of the Fed's balance sheet?

A: Our new Chairman has initiated a task force to study the balance sheet of the Federal Reserve system, how it has changed over time and what the appropriate stance of the balance sheet is. They're supposed to be reporting back to us by the end of the year and I would not want to get out in front of that. So I am going to remain agnostic about these very important questions. My answer is very much stay tuned, because my mind is totally open.

Q: How much does staff research inform your decisions?

A: An FOMC meeting always begins with a presentation by the staff of economic and monetary conditions in the United States and abroad, and I value that information very highly. They give us multiple scenarios, trying to take into account a wide variety of possible scenarios of where the economy may go and the shocks that might hit us. Independently I'm having contact meetings, so a part of the job is really one of synthesis.

Q: How do you synthesize the sea of information when making high-stakes decisions under uncertainty?

A: It was a lot easier when I was an academic, because as academics we tend to isolate one particular shock or channel or transmission mechanism. Models are by definition false, but they're incredibly useful for organizing one's thinking. When you are an actual policymaker, there are multiple things going on all at once, and I have to weigh the totality of them. Right now we have the AI buildout, energy price shocks, trade policy changes, and demographic changes in the labor market. So the challenge is to say, with all of that going on, what is the best decision that I can make right now, given the vast amount of uncertainty that I face, and knowing that not everybody's going to love what you do. Given the information that I have right now, this is the best decision that I can make right now, and you have to believe in that and vote accordingly.
