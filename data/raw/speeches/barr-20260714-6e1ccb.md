## 1. AI upside for low- and moderate-income households

**Moderator:**

Michael, thank you so much for that speech. I feel like I appreciate the choice to talk about inequality and to just dive into AI. So, I wonder if we could just start there. You laid out a lot, but when you think about the lens of people living on low and moderate income, is there any piece of all that you laid out that you're especially bullish about? Let's start with the upside.

**Michael Barr:**

Yeah, Tim, first of all, let me just say it's a great pleasure to be back together with you here. And I remember 25 years ago when you and Peter launched D2D and what became Commonwealth, and the work that you've been doing over the last quarter century now has really been impactful. So great to be back here with you.

Let me say, I think that if properly deployed, artificial intelligence can help low-income people in lots of different ways.

We'll talk, I think, in a little bit about this in the finance area, but more generally, thinking about workers. Let's say you're a worker with low English-language proficiency. AI can help you make that leap to using English much more quickly than if you had to take the full time to learn English as a second or third or fourth language. So that's an example.

AI can help workers become much more productive and thus increase the possibility or probability that they'll get a wage boost from that productivity.

For example, there's been lots of work on workers in call centers. And it's true that AI might diminish the number of call center workers, but it's also the case that the call center workers who remain doing that will be much more productive and be able to earn higher wages because of that productivity.

So these are the kinds of things you can go kind of not only job by job, but taking the job apart and looking at tasks within jobs, and ask: how is AI going to make me more productive as a worker and therefore enable me to advance in my career in ways that, if I had lower levels of education, might be very, very difficult?

## 2. Guard rails for AI

**Moderator:**

No, I appreciate that, and it mirrors your comments that people have an opportunity, if I hear you right, to really increase their income in a way that might have been more difficult in the past.

Maybe let's take the flip side, and in particular to put a finer point on it. Are there any — we hear this phrase “guard rails” — are there any guard rails, whether those be policy or others, that you especially crave for as we head into this new world?

**Michael Barr:**

Well, the world is changing rapidly, and I think we need to be supportive of that innovation and, at the same time, as you said, have clear guard rails.

So one important issue for people is privacy. AI developments may make it more difficult to protect one's privacy. It's already hard today, but may make it more difficult to protect one's privacy. That's an issue to worry about.

We might see, in the credit space, AI expanding access to credit in new and innovative ways, using alternative data or existing data in new ways. But it's also the case that AI, if there aren't really good guard rails, might reinforce the biases that we already have in our society and result in worse outcomes in terms of credit access.

So that's a second area where we would really need clear guard rails before you'd want a financial institution to use AI in credit decision-making — use advanced generative AI. Obviously, for 50 years people have been using machine learning in all kinds of interesting ways, but I'm really focused now on generative AI and this latest wave of AI.

So privacy is an important area. Bias is an important area.

A third area you can think of as values alignment. So making sure, as a user or as a society or at any level in between, that AI is acting according to the values of the individual and of society and not getting out of alignment with those values. That's an important thing that the builders of AI work on and are working on, but also we as a country, as a society, need to think about how those guard rails are reinforced and built in.

There are all kinds of issues that might arise with AI and financial stability. For example, some uses of AI in trading — again, AI is being used in trading today — but some forms of generative AI that might be used in trading could accelerate runs, could lead to herd behavior, might result in collusion among AI machines.

So it's really critical that we also have good guard rails on issues like financial stability and market integrity. So these are some of the examples.

## 3. Trust, adoption and access to AI tools

**Moderator:**

Those all resonate. Let's pick up on something from your opening remarks which I was really struck by. Sometimes you have an anecdotal experience and then someone delivers data that really makes the point.

What I heard you say is that people who have less education and probably less income are using some of these frontier generative AI models less. And I think you even made the point that they're seeing less effect in the way that they're using them.

And that leads me to this question: we see in our work that there is quite understandable mistrust of institutions, of technologies, of systems, and that can be a factor that holds people back from trying things.

So I'm curious for your thoughts on that and what you think we can do to build that trust or earn the trust, so we see everybody at least getting the same chance to benefit.

**Michael Barr:**

Yeah, it's interesting. I do think AI has the potential to increase trust appropriately in the financial system. There are lots of careful words in that. It has the opportunity to increase that trust appropriately.

So what do I mean by that? AI is a tool and, like other tools, it can be used to increase trust in inappropriate ways — for you to trust a scammer, for example. And so AI can be used for fraud. We're seeing that already. Or cyber attacks. So you might inappropriately increase trust.

But I'm talking about appropriately increasing trust in institutions that should be trusted, in processes that should be trusted. AI might help overcome some of those trust barriers.

There is some sense in which AI can connect with and respond to individuals in a tailored, individualized way so that the person reaching out to the AI for help is being responded to as an individual human being. And in the same way that a one-to-one conversation with another human might help build trust, having that kind of tailored response that treats each individual for their own individual selves might also be a way to build that trust.

Or I mentioned before language can be a barrier to trust, and AI can bridge that quite readily. These are just a couple examples.

I know, Tim, you've spent a lot of time thinking about trust in the financial system. So how do you think about that same set of issues?

## 4. Trust in technology and user experience

**Moderator:**

Yeah. Well, I can share a little bit of research we had a chance to do. This was really looking at banks and chatbots, but I think the lessons translate. Some of what we saw is what you already highlighted and might be common sense.

People want to be sure that their data is safe. Who among us doesn't want to be sure our data is safe? We know there are bad actors out there. So that made a lot of sense.

Another thing we saw is that people tend to trust technology if they trust the provider of that technology. In this case that was banks, and we saw a very high correlation between how people felt about their bank and whether or not they trusted the technology. I think that finding probably translates to whether it's the technology firms or other financial service firms delivering the technology.

But some of the things that were maybe not immediately obvious, that were really important: people had a very high sense of whether or not the technology was useful and relevant to them and the experience.

And again, how many of us can relate to this, especially if you think about an old-school chatbot? You engage and you just get something back that's just not all that helpful. “Oh, I could have done that myself,” or “I can sense I'm in some kind of decision tree and I've been pushed to an answer.”

People are very perceptive about that, even if they don't have a lot of money, and they go, wow, this is not for me.

So I think that's important to your point about personalization and tailoring. People needed it to be useful to them.

The other thing that we learned, which again maybe surprising, maybe not, is that people need to feel that they're not being judged. **Michael Barr:**

Yeah, that's exactly right. We need to see broad adoption in order for it to potentially have that positive effect that both of us have been describing.

And I very much resonated with your point about individual capability. Most people want to feel empowered by the technology they use to become smarter, better, faster, stronger — whatever your definition is.

And so I do think that's a potential for the tool, again, if appropriately designed and used.

## 5. Financial education and capability

**Moderator:**

I appreciated in your remarks you talked about the need for greater curiosity and some other things.

Just briefly, I think a lot of us in this room who care about these issues, and your long history, are aware of many resources that have gone into financial education, financial capability building. And I'm curious how that needs to evolve as the tools and the challenges evolve. Do you have any thoughts on that?

**Michael Barr:**

Yeah. Well, first let me say just a word about curiosity. When young people ask me about their careers and what they should study — I spent a lot of time in academia and mentored a lot of young people — the first thing I say to them is the most important skill you have is a skill of having a sense of curiosity, because that's something you can carry with you your whole life.

And if you have that, you're going to learn new skills all along the way. One of the amazing things about being here at the Federal Reserve, I know when I come to work in the morning that by the end of the day, I'm going to learn something new. That's pretty amazing. So that's the curiosity.

Now, your second question is about whether there is something that we want to be thinking about to evolve financial capability, financial education, training, so that we're actually setting people up.

I think financial education has moved already a lot over the last 30 years. It used to be the case that people talked about financial literacy and the model in people's head was: you bring people into a classroom and you teach them a bunch of things, and then they go out and use it in the world.

Over time it became apparent that that was not the smartest way of thinking about financial education. And the field moved to effectively financial capability and empowering individuals using a moment of decision, using behavioral insights, and using some education to change outcomes.

I think generative AI will accelerate that movement in that direction because now you can use that moment of contact and that behavioral insight in a way that is even more tailored to the particular circumstances of the individual being affected.

And that makes it more likely that you'll get a better outcome. We originally started talking about financial literacy many decades ago because we wanted better outcomes for people, and now you can get to those outcomes potentially in a more direct way.

## 6. Confidence, subjective barriers and belonging

**Moderator:**

I appreciate that. I guess the one additional thought I'd just add is that, again in this long history, out of an abundance of earnestness often we have wanted to make sure people are equipped with all the right facts and knowledge, and we've learned that's not enough.

I think one frontier we want to be thinking about is a little bit what we talked about just a minute ago. We need people to both have the basis to be confident and to build that confidence in their own ability to navigate. And that's really tricky. I'm not sure exactly we know how to do it, but I think putting that on the agenda.

You and I talked a little bit about this. We do a lot of work trying to support people who want to become investors for the first time. And through that, we learned that one thing they'll say if you sit long enough is, “I know what to do, and I actually have a little bit of money, and I know what tool to access, but I still don't feel like I belong in that world.”

And those kinds of subjective realities are invisible barriers, and I think we want to figure out how we make progress on those.

If I could shift, we're thinking, at least in our shop, and I think you do too, a lot about the financial lives of people who don't earn a lot of money. One thing that just comes up over and over again is having a little bit of slack, of just making it through the week or the month.

Do you have any thoughts about what role generative AI can play in increasing that slack for folks?

**Michael Barr:**

It's a great question. I wrote a book about this topic back in 2012 called *No Slack*, the financial lives of low-income Americans. And the reason I wrote that book is because I had seen the lived experiences of low-income people who were really smart and were hardworking and were just trying to juggle in an impossible way their income and expenses.

And that lack of slack made it much more likely that a small problem would lead to a big one.

More broadly, Sendhil Mullainathan and Eldar Shafir wrote a book a few years back called *Scarcity*, where they explore that concept not only for people who don't have any money and therefore don't have financial slack, but people who have scarcity of other kinds.

You can have a scarcity in time and therefore not be able to do the things that you need to do. Or you can have a scarcity of focus. There's only so much that we can focus on at any given time. And you may need to focus on one set of issues and therefore be blindsided by a problem that comes up.

So AI could help with those kinds of scarcities. On the financial side, AI could help individuals better plan, better anticipate problems in the future, and therefore be better able to insure against those difficulties, whether through savings or through insurance or through credit to smooth through those difficulties.

So AI might be sort of an uber budgeting tool for low-income people.

It could turn out that AI changes the financial slack people have directly by giving them an outlet to be more productive and therefore to earn higher income and therefore to be less stressed in the lives that they live.

And AI might help also in the scarcity of time and of focus. So if you have scarce time and focus and you're really focused appropriately on doing your job, for example, as an auto mechanic, you don't have much time to think about these other things. Well, AI can be doing that work for you.

While you're doing your day job, it's doing its job of paying attention to this financial issue you're worried about and spending time solving that issue.

So it really can help with, I think, all three kinds of scarcities we've been talking about. And I know you've also been working on this for 25 years. So how do you think about it?

## 12. Financial inclusion progress and risks from AI

**Audience:**

Thank you. Kate Han from the Boston Fed. In the opening remarks, you said that there have been improvements in the financial inclusion field. So I wanted to ask which one of those are you most impressed with and which one are you most concerned might be undermined by developments in AI?

**Michael Barr:**

Well, I think broadly speaking — this is a work in progress — but broadly speaking over the last 30 years, there has been more attention paid by the financial services sector to developing products and services that could meet the needs of low- and moderate-income households.

That's a very broad statement, but that's over the sweep of the last 30 years. That general movement has been positive and I think needs to continue.

Imagine a world in which financial institutions structured their products in the first instance with intentionality to serve low- and moderate-income people and then thought about how it could be adapted to rich people. That would be huge, right? It would be a big change in our society.

So I think the closer that we can get towards that goal, the better off we will be at improving financial inclusion.

I think AI can help with that, again if used properly, by helping design products at a cheaper cost and monitor those products and their usage at a cheaper cost using more data.

So many of you in this room are working on financial health metrics. I think AI can help advance financial health metrics both in terms of data and in terms of modeling, and can then tailor the output from that to particular product design that could better serve low- and moderate-income people.
