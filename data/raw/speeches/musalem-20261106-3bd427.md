Good morning. I am very pleased to be with you today in this amazing institution.

Since moving to St. Louis, I have experienced the incredible assets that make this community such a terrific place to live: this world-class art museum; an unbelievable zoo; the grandeur of Forest Park; and most importantly, toasted ravs. My family and I are now fans of these St. Louis traditions, and many more.

I am in my third year at the St. Louis Fed, and I have been impressed by the engagement of our region’s business community. You believe in St. Louis and are another real asset for all of us who live and work here. I have a similar impression about other engaged business communities across the District we serve.

This morning, I wanted to share a few thoughts about a topic that is vital to the American economy and all of us — the importance of a decentralized central bank and the role you play in it. I also want to discuss one thing that I believe could be a challenge for the Fed in the future.

Let me stress at the outset that these are my views and not necessarily those of my Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) colleagues.[1]

## Decentralized by Design

The U.S. economy is incredibly diverse and dynamic. That is why monetary policy must reflect the experiences of people, businesses and communities from all across the country. Regional Reserve banks and the people we serve both play an important part in that work.

As your regional Reserve Bank, the St. Louis Fed serves more than 15 million people across all or parts of seven states. We have branch locations and teams in Little Rock, Louisville and Memphis.

The Fed’s structure reflects work done many years ago to make sure the American people had a voice in monetary policy.

The Federal Reserve System is the nation’s third attempt at a central bank. There were a number of shortcomings with the first two Banks of the United States. One important issue was a widespread perception that they represented the monied classes, predominantly in the largest cities on the East Coast, and not the interests of all Americans.

So, after a substantial amount of wrangling, effort and compromise, Congress passed the Federal Reserve Act of 1913 and created a uniquely American central bank. They established a federated bank that was decentralized by design and that has served the nation well for more than 112 years.

Key to that successful compromise was the creation of regional Reserve banks across the country. Among other things, those Reserve banks were intended to represent the economic experiences of the people in their districts, help provide payment services and maintain a stable banking system in the communities they served. America’s new central bank was designed to make sure it worked well for people on Main Street, not just Wall Street.[2]

However, the young Federal Reserve experienced some growing pains. It soon became apparent that coordinated action would be necessary to have an effective monetary policy. In the 1930s, a lack of coordination among the Reserve banks hampered the Fed’s ability to mount a vigorous response to the Great Depression.

To address that issue, Congress acted again in 1935 by creating the Federal Open Market Committee to set and carry out a national monetary policy. The FOMC was designed to incorporate both local and national perspectives by including regional Reserve bank presidents and members of the Board of Governors in Washington, D.C.

Through the FOMC, Congress ensured that views from across the country would be heard and would influence the nation’s monetary policy decisions.[3]

Today, Reserve banks have many important roles. Those include: meeting banks’ currency needs by providing them with about 29 billion Federal Reserve notes a year; advancing a reliable and efficient payments system that moves about $5 trillion dollars every day; serving as the fiscal agent (or the banker) for the U.S. Treasury; and conducting world-class economic research to inform monetary policy decisions. The St. Louis Fed’s Research team has long been one of the world’s thought leaders on monetary policy.

Reserve banks also promote a safe and sound financial system. That includes working under the Board of Governors’ legal authority to supervise approximately 3,600 financial institutions, which hold more than $30 trillion in assets. Doing that work through Reserve banks in a federated system brings bank supervision closer to the communities we serve. It also allows Reserve bank supervision staff to use their understanding of local economic conditions in doing their work.

In addition to all of that, one of the most important and enduring functions for Reserve banks continues to be bringing the perspectives of people from across our districts when we make monetary policy decisions. A substantial part of my job is to bring your views to the FOMC table.

Congress has given the Fed a dual mandate: promoting maximum employment and stable prices. To do that well, teams from the St. Louis Fed and other Reserve banks regularly seek to learn how people in our districts are affecting and being affected by the economy.

The stories you tell us today will become the statistics we pore over months from now. You help us maintain an informed view of the economy in real time.

My colleagues and I share those personal stories during FOMC meetings when we consider policy options. Information that comes from Americans of all walks of life — in every part of the country — helps us avoid groupthink and allows us to make more robust and representative policy decisions.

## Cornerstone of Monetary Policy Independence

Let me now say a few words about the Fed’s monetary policy independence. Research over time shows that countries have stronger economies and less inflation when they have central banks that set monetary policy independent of political influence.

Congress gave the Fed its decentralized structure to help ensure Washington politics would not dominate U.S. monetary policy.[4] By giving voice to the concerns and perspectives of the public, Reserve banks serve as a fundamental cornerstone of U.S. monetary policy independence.

Another way Reserve banks are connected to their district is through each bank’s board of directors. Reserve banks’ head office boards are made up of local leaders from their district. Directors oversee the selection of the bank’s president and regularly provide invaluable economic insights, as well as governance over strategy, operations, finance and risk.

Some of you have served on the St. Louis Fed’s board of directors, and we are extremely grateful for your leadership.

## Modernized with Local Control

A decentralized Fed has been a key part of America’s economic success. But, just like your companies and organizations, we need to continually evolve. The Fed must constantly work to see how we can be most efficient and effective in our service to the American people.

Early last year, the Federal Reserve System announced plans to reduce the overall size of its employee base by about 10%. That work is nearly done.

The Fed is also in the process of consolidating certain functions. Historically, the Fed’s regional bank structure has been designed as 12 different verticals — each reporting to that Reserve bank’s president and board of directors.

Today, where it makes sense, we are implementing a more horizontal approach. We don’t have to do human resources, procurement or information technology 12 different times, in 12 different ways, across 12 different banks. Consolidating some of that work into a few banks will allow us to remain as efficient as possible.

We have an example of how a previous consolidation has worked well right here in St. Louis. As I mentioned earlier, Reserve banks serve as the banker for the U.S. Treasury. For many years, the St. Louis Fed has managed that relationship with Treasury and helped to coordinate the work done by six Reserve banks. That joint effort right here in St. Louis demonstrates the benefits of coordinating key functions.

## Ongoing Challenge

All of us at the Fed are fortunate to be stewards of an incredible institution. When it is time for any of us to leave, we all want the Fed to be even better than we found it. So, I think often about what the Fed will need in the future to most effectively work for the American people.

I believe that if we lose our focus on representing the economic experiences of all Americans, we will have failed in a key part of our work. If we become less connected to the people we serve, we will have failed. If we don’t regularly communicate how our work — over time — makes the American economy healthier, we will have failed to live up to an important obligation we have.

I want you to know that across the Reserve banks, we are doing more today to strengthen the public’s involvement. Last year alone the St. Louis Fed engaged with approximately 50,000 people directly and millions more online. I personally engaged with more than 4,000 people across our District. And, as you may know, the St. Louis Fed is home to FRED — Federal Reserve Economic Data. FRED alone has 25 million human users, and each year we get about 3.7 billion requests for data. Each of those engagements makes us better at what we do.

So, when you think about the St. Louis Fed, remember you are part of it. When you hear people talk about Fed independence and the ability to set monetary policy without political interference, remember: They are talking about a system that depends on you.

Please continue to engage with us, respond to our Beige Book surveys, serve on our boards and councils, and join us in person to share your insights.

Reserve banks are your voice in the Federal Reserve System. Thank you for being part of America’s decentralized central bank.

Now, I look forward to taking questions about our work and my perspectives on today’s economy and monetary policy.
