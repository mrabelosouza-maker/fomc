# Yahoo Finance Interview: More Than One Hike to Bring Down Inflation — hammack — 2026-08-10

## Highlights

Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, in a Yahoo Finance interview, says she anticipates that
more than one interest-rate hike will be required to rein in what she calls broadening inflation.
She does not view the current 3.50–3.75% range as "meaningfully restricting" the economy, says
"now is the time to act," and dismisses the July payroll loss of 23,000 as a reason to shift focus
away from inflation. She also pushes back on the idea that higher bond yields can do the Fed's
work for it: "markets are a complement for the Fed. They're not a substitute."

---

Cleveland Federal Reserve president Beth Hammack said Monday she anticipates more than one
interest rate hike will be required to rein in what she calls broadening inflation.

"I would say in general, one 25 basis point move probably doesn't do a whole lot for the economy,"
Hammack told Yahoo Finance in an interview. "So it's probably some number of [movements]. But I
don't want to prejudge what that number is going to be."

She added, "I don't know exactly where we will end."

Hammack dissented at the Fed's July policy meeting when the Federal Open Market Committee held
interest rates steady, preferring to have raised rates by a quarter percentage point.

She said she does not think interest rates in the range of 3.5% to 3.75% are "meaningfully
restricting" the economy right now.

"When I'm talking to businesses, I'm not hearing that they're sensing any restraint from
investments in growth based on where interest rates are," Hammack said. "So to me that says that
now is the time to act."

She cautioned that the longer the Fed waits, the longer it misses its inflation goal of 2% and the
harder inflation will be to bring back down.

Hammack likened raising rates to pumping the brakes before a stop sign to glide to a stop, rather
than slamming on the brakes to halt price growth.

"So I think that now is the time for us to start acting, to start bringing more restraint into
policy," she said.

The Fed's preferred inflation gauge — the Personal Consumption Expenditures index — stood at 3.3%
as of June on a core basis, which excludes volatile food and energy prices. Another measure, the
Consumer Price Index, was 2.6% on a core basis for June, and economists expect that it ticked down
again in July to 2.5% and 0.2%. Investors will get that reading on Wednesday. If core CPI meets
expectations, that would mark two straight months of declining inflation.

"Nothing would make me feel better than to be wrong, that we need to change the stance of policy
to help bring inflation back to target," Hammack said. "But from where I sit, I just don't see it
coming back on its own."

Hammack noted that the July jobs report — which showed a surprising loss of 23,000 jobs for the
month — doesn't take her focus away from inflation. She noted that over the past 12 months,
payrolls have been adding an average of 20,000 to 25,000 jobs, and said the unemployment rate of
4.1% is right around her estimate of full employment.

"I'm still not seeing a problem" with the job market, she said.

## Markets are a complement, not a substitute

At the July policy meeting, Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh repeatedly pointed to bond yields that had
moved higher since the last meeting and suggested that's a good thing. That has caused confusion
in markets and led to some loss of confidence in whether the Fed has a credible strategy to bring
down inflation or wants the markets to do its work for it.

"Markets are a complement for the Fed," Hammack said. "They're not a substitute. We have to stand
behind our words with our actions when appropriate. And so certainly I'm going to factor in how
markets are performing, how they're interpreting what we're saying, but ultimately that's not a
substitute for us taking action when it's necessary."

Hammack underscored that the Fed holds itself accountable to its 2% inflation target based on core
PCE. She also noted that she thinks the Fed's credibility has been built through transparency.

"It's built by us being clear with the American public about what our intentions are about how…
we're going to go about delivering on that commitment of 2% inflation," she said. "But that doesn't
mean giving forward guidance. That means helping to explain our reaction function. It means telling
the public how any new piece of data that comes in will impact our overall mosaic and will help
inform our decision-making."

Hammack pointed to research that shows that when business owners and individuals understand the
Fed's framework behind its decisions, they're able to make better decisions for themselves.
"That's really where I think the credibility comes in, is being transparent and clear about what
our objective is. Our objective is to deliver that 2% inflation. And so we need to make sure that
we are able to do that," she said.

When pressed on markets being confused by the Fed's reaction function — essentially how the central
bank reacts to the economy and inflation — Hammack said that the markets can be uncertain about a
number of different things.

"I don't think it's just about the Fed," she said. "But all of that comes together and it doesn't
relieve or increase the burden on the Fed to make sure that we're delivering on both sides of our
mandate."
